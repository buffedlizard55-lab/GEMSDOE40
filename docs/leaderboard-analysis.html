The metric explains the tradeoff precisely

Let T be distance-weighted true-positive credit, F the weighted false-positive mass, and G the number of truth pixels. The published metric simplifies to:

D = T / (0.2T + 0.2F + 0.8G + ε)
Removing predictions improves D exactly when:
(1 − 0.2D) × lost TP credit < 0.2D × saved FP mass

At D=0.2778, losing less than 0.05883 units of TP credit per unit of saved FP mass improves the score. Thus pruning redundant/incorrect predictions can help without discovering anything new. It does not justify deleting all catalogue-adjacent structures: the organizer confirms that new faults may lie within 300 m of known traces.

Can we beat 0.2778—or 0.3345?

It is a reasonable research goal, not a defensible promise from the available data. Holding F and G fixed, moving from 0.2778 to 0.3345 would require about 21.87% more weighted TP credit. That is an algebraic scenario, not a predicted score; a real model changes both T and F.

H4 deliberately tries new source geometry instead of copying and thinning H33. It passes novelty but obtains only 0.006902 on the local proxy versus H33-B2’s 0.091550. We therefore have no evidence that H4 beats the owner’s best. It is not submitted.

The next high-value work

  1. Replace the circular/impossible promotion instrument in a separately preregistered experiment with independent, whole-fault-system validation.
  2. Test finite-step gravity models and survey-datum-corrected source localization; use synthetic known-depth controls before region-wide inference.
  3. Separate detection skill from output mass. Compare methods at matched confidence mass and evaluate native continuous outputs, without reusing the test fold to tune emission.
  4. For geothermal interpretation, require independent geologic/thermal corroboration rather than equating every potential-field contact with a permeable fault.
